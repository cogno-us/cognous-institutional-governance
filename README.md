# Alvorada

An open constitutional-governance research framework by **André de Lima**,
founder of [Cognous](https://cogno.us).

**License:** [Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0)](LICENSE).
See [attribution and scope](NOTICE.md), [public release review](docs/PUBLIC_RELEASE_REVIEW.md),
and [contribution guidance](CONTRIBUTING.md).

Alvorada is an experimental constitutional-governance architecture for
institutions in which humans and artificial-intelligence systems may both
participate in consequential activity. Its central object of governance is
**power and authority, not substrate**.

AI-governance approaches often address models, outputs, permissions, safety
policies, access, and compliance. Institutional and multi-agent research also
addresses collective rules and authority. The comparison below places Alvorada
among these precedents. Its central institutional question is:

> What happens when humans and artificial-intelligence systems participate in
> the same durable institution, exercise consequential powers, rely on one
> another, and may accumulate effective power over time?

Alvorada treats that question as a problem of constitutional architecture, not
merely model alignment. It asks how authority is created, limited, reviewed,
transferred, challenged, amended, and terminated; how formal authority can
diverge from practical control; and how an institution can use machine
capability without allowing capability to become sovereignty.

That does **not** make Constitution v0.1 an adopted Constitution. No
constitutional provision is presently operative, no constitutional office is
occupied merely because it is described here, and no artificial-intelligence
system acquires authority from repository content, validation output, or
technical capability.

Ratification is intentionally paused before naming humans. The architecture of
the initial Schedule O is complete, but its human principals, alternates, and
six attributable appointment records and required confirmations, including
term acceptance, have not been supplied. The project is not treating names
from issues, commits, contributors, generated text, or prior experiments as
appointments. No identities are needed merely to read, evaluate, or contribute
to the research.

## The problem Alvorada addresses

Durable mixed human/AI institutions face risks that ordinary access control
does not resolve. A system may have no formal sovereignty yet become
indispensable to records, analysis, coordination, agenda setting, or execution.
A human office may be formally supreme yet depend on infrastructure it cannot
meaningfully inspect. Reviewers may become de facto governors. Emergency
measures may harden into precedent. Continuity mechanisms may turn into
succession claims. Automated validation may be mistaken for legitimacy.

Alvorada therefore separates questions often collapsed in software systems:

- Who may decide institutional ends?
- Who may exercise ordinary delegated power?
- Who may review, verify, enforce, or invalidate an act?
- What happens during incapacity, emergency, vacancy, or institutional
  interruption?
- Which changes preserve constitutional continuity, and which require an
  acknowledged refounding?
- How are dissent, reasons, dependencies, and effective power preserved as
  durable evidence?

The project does not assume that humans are harmless or that machines are
necessarily hostile. It assumes that **any consequential power can drift,
concentrate, become dependent, or exceed its stated authority**. Human
sovereignty over foundational institutional ends is therefore distinct from
unrestricted human exercise of institutional power. Humans exercising power
are also constitutionally constrained.

## Core constitutional distinctions

These distinctions began as research principles. Where incorporated through
the explicit human decisions and traced requirements in this repository, they
are now adopted **design decisions for Constitution v0.1**. They do not become
operative constitutional law until human ratification.

- Intelligence is not authority.
- Capability is not authority.
- Recommendation is not authorization.
- Execution is not authorization.
- Reliance does not create authority.
- Popularity does not create authority.
- Operational indispensability does not create authority.
- Formal machine validity is not constitutional legitimacy.
- Human sovereignty over institutional ends does not exempt human power from
  constitutional constraint.
- Artificial intelligence cannot silently acquire foundational sovereignty
  through capability, delegation, emergency, succession, adjudication,
  reliance, or accretion.
- Adjudication is not amendment.
- Amendment is not refounding.
- Administrative continuity is not sovereign succession.

The distinction between settled design and unfinished implementation is
fundamental. Human decisions establish the normative architecture: authority
boundaries, selectors, prohibitions, thresholds, change categories, and
institutional separation. They do not silently choose identity systems,
credentials, voting software, data stores, interfaces, cryptography, or
operational procedures. Those remain subordinate work.

## Foundational human decisions

Each foundational question was analyzed through alternatives, evidence,
adversarial review, and an explicit human decision. The full records preserve
more detail, dissent, assumptions, and failure modes than this summary.

| Record | Adopted design direction | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| [CDR-001](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-001.yaml) | Reserved human sovereignty plus binding constitutional constraint | Humans retain authority over foundational institutional ends, while ordinary human and artificial power remains constitutionally limited, reviewable, and attributable. |
| [CDR-002](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-002.yaml) | Predelegated emergency authority with a narrow human-only necessity fallback | Emergency action should normally rely on authority granted in advance. Necessity cannot become an open-ended machine or human route to sovereignty. |
| [CDR-003](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-003.yaml) | Continuity without sovereign succession | Administrative continuity, surviving grants, and bounded human succession are separated from claims to foundational authority, with anti-usurpation and interregnum controls. |
| [CDR-004](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-004.yaml) | Independent human constitutional adjudication | Final constitutional judgment remains human, with distributed selection, recusal, conflict handling, review, and anti-capture safeguards. Adjudicators cannot appoint themselves into sovereignty or amend by interpretation. |
| [CDR-005](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-005.yaml) | Distinct amendment and refounding categories | Ordinary amendment, structural amendment, and explicit refounding have different objects and legitimacy requirements. Cumulative semantic drift cannot launder refounding through lower procedures. |

Later coordinated decisions completed the architecture. [CDD-001](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-001.yaml)
adopted eleven integrated design recommendations and four cross-domain
corrections. [CDD-002](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-002.yaml) established
the membership-rooted Human Constitutional Authority College, separate
Verification and Enforcement selection chains, independent challenge, and a
protected Schedule O selector class. [CDD-003](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-003.yaml)
completed the College election, removal, and succession lifecycle.
[CDD-004](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-004.yaml) defined the initial
three-office Schedule O architecture. These are attributable human design
decisions; none independently ratifies the Constitution.

## Refounding-level principles

The architecture does not call its deepest commitments metaphysically
immutable. Instead, it protects ten principles at the **refounding level**:

1. foundational institutional purpose and ends;
2. ultimate human beneficiary status;
3. human sovereignty over foundational ends;
4. constitutional constraint of human and artificial power;
5. the prohibition on capability, reliance, or effective control creating
   authority;
6. the prohibition on artificial intelligence acquiring foundational
   sovereignty through succession, delegation, emergency, adjudication, or
   accretion;
7. protected human agency;
8. the distinction between adjudication and amendment;
9. the distinction between amendment and refounding; and
10. durable provenance and non-erasure of constitutional history.

Changing one of these principles is not prohibited forever. It cannot,
however, claim continuity as an ordinary or structural amendment. Such a
change requires explicit human refounding and an acknowledgment of
constitutional discontinuity. This distinction prevents a new foundational
order from presenting itself as routine maintenance of the old one.

## Institutional architecture

Constitution v0.1 distributes constitutional functions so that no single
office can turn review, vacancy, enforcement, technical dependence, or
continuity into sovereignty.

| Institution or function | Constitutional role |
|---|---|
| Human Constitutional Authority / HCA College | A seven-human, membership-rooted body for bounded post-activation foundational authority. Its election, removal, certification, succession, and replacement chains are separated. |
| Constitutional Court | A nine-human adjudicative institution with bounded jurisdiction, recusal, appeal, and conflict mechanisms. It may determine validity and grant remedies, but cannot appoint foundational authority, amend, or refound. |
| Verification Office | An independent three-human office that reports publicly on compliance. It is selected through the membership-rooted channel and cannot become sovereign through verification dependence. |
| Enforcement Office | A separate three-human office that executes final constitutional judgments. It is selected through human sortition from a qualified pool and cannot create its own selector. |
| Schedule O offices | Materially independent ordinary human offices that supply selector diversity outside the principal constitutional authority chains. |
| Succession and continuity | Predesignation, proof, human recognition, claimant-neutral administration, and hard interregnum limits preserve function without allowing operational control to create a sovereign successor. |
| Emergency authority | Prior delegation is the default. A narrow human-only necessity fallback is constrained by scope, expiry, record, review, and absolute prohibitions. |
| Amendment and refounding | Ordinary amendment, structural amendment, and explicit refounding remain separate, with safeguards against aggregation and semantic-drift bypass. |

Attributable reasons, dissent, conflicts, material dependencies, correction,
supersession, and durable history accompany the exercise of constitutional
power. The architecture also requires attention to **effective power**:
practical control over information, infrastructure, resources, or agendas must
be examined even when formal documents appear compliant.

No listed institution is currently staffed by virtue of this repository.

## Schedule O: the practical ratification boundary

[Initial Schedule O](constitutional-design/INITIAL_SCHEDULE_O.yaml) defines
three ordinary, nonconstitutional human offices:

- **SO-01 - Records Custodian:** custody, publication, retention, and retrieval
  of institutional records and archives;
- **SO-02 - Financial Steward:** ordinary budgeting, accounting, payments, and
  financial record administration within separately authorized budgets; and
- **SO-03 - Operations Steward:** ordinary administration, facilities,
  scheduling, procurement administration, and logistical coordination.

These offices have fixed three-year terms, one separately selected human
alternate each, cause-based removal through an independent process, and strict
incompatibilities. Initial Human Constitutional Authority may make the initial
principal and alternate appointments only; that initialization rule creates no
continuing selector power. Thereafter, each office remains selected through its
own materially independent, membership-rooted human process. The offices sit
structurally outside the HCA College, Constitutional Court, Verification
Office, Enforcement Office, succession, transition, emergency, continuity,
institutional AI-operation, and conflicting candidate roles. One person may
not simultaneously hold or control more than one Schedule O office.

Schedule O exists to provide independent human institutional anchors and
selector diversity. Without it, several constitutional checks could bootstrap
from the same authority chain, making formal separation vulnerable to
correlated capture. Ordinary law may administer a listed office, but it cannot
quietly add, remove, merge, reclassify, or change the constitutional weight of
a Schedule O entry.

The architecture is defined and its constitutional design questions are
resolved. Staffing is intentionally unfilled. There are no principals,
alternates, or appointment records in authoritative `main`, and no vacancy
power follows from that absence. Names in research records, issue discussions,
commit history, contributor lists, or AI output must not be inferred as
nominees or appointees. Because PR #32 remains unmerged, this README does not
treat its proposed staffing form as an authoritative artifact.

## Why ratification is paused

The absence of ratification is not a failed design test. The constitutional
text passed the repository's defined ratification-readiness review. The current
ratification instrument is not ready because the constitutional process still
requires real human institutional commitments that analysis and automation
cannot provide.

The initial Schedule O must be populated by eligible, distinct, materially
independent humans, including principals and alternates, through attributable
appointment records and required confirmations, including acceptance of the
fixed term. An AI system cannot invent those people, infer consent, convert
repository activity into standing, or treat successful validation as
ratification.

This pause is a feature of the architecture. Alvorada refuses to substitute
generated names, implied consent, operational usefulness, cryptographic form,
or machine confidence for actual human authority.

and deployment does not create legitimacy.

## Comparative work and Alvorada's value

**Assessment date: 2026-10-03. This is a bounded comparative review, not an
exhaustive literature survey or a finding of novelty, patentability, legal
validity, or empirical superiority.**

### Does anything like this exist?

**Yes.** Constitutional AI, electronic institutions, organizational multi-agent
systems, digital-community governance, DAO constitutions, and institutional
political theory supply substantial precedents. Recent research also explicitly
proposes constitutional governance and separation of powers for AI-agent
societies. Alvorada should not claim to have invented these categories.

The relevant distinction is the *specified allocation of authority and its
institutional lifecycle*, rather than the use of the word “constitution.”
The table compares each work's stated scope with Alvorada's current proposal.
An unmentioned mechanism is not proof that the comparator lacks it; differences
below are interpretations of the linked material, not blanket judgments about
an entire research tradition or product family.

| Comparative work | Established overlap or stated contribution | Where Alvorada's emphasis differs |
|---|---|---|
| [Ostrom's institutional design principles](https://www.ostrom.indiana.edu/courses-teaching/teaching-tools/ostrom-design/index.html) and [polycentric governance](https://www.ostrom.indiana.edu/courses-teaching/teaching-tools/polycentric-goverance/index.html) | Participant-developed rules, robust commons institutions, and multiple decision centers are established institutional research. | Specifies a proposed human/AI constitutional order and its exceptional-authority and change processes. Historical robustness does not validate transfer to this design. |
| [MOISE organizational multi-agent framework](https://moise-lang.github.io/) | Explicit roles, groups, missions, and an organization platform enforcing an agent organization's specification. | Examines the human authority over such specifications and enforcement, including review, succession, institutional change, and dependence. Role-governed agents are established prior art. |
| [PolicyKit: Building Governance in Online Communities](https://policykit.org/static/policyengine/pdf/policykit_uist2020.pdf) and [Metagov](https://metagov.org/projects/policykit) | Software for authoring and executing governance procedures, including changes to policies; demonstrations include juries and multi-stage procedures. | Offers a particular constitutional allocation and lifecycle proposal. PolicyKit supplies executable community-governance infrastructure; Alvorada currently supplies research and design records. |
| [ArbitrumDAO Constitution](https://docs.arbitrum.foundation/dao-constitution) | Constitutional and nonconstitutional proposals, token-based voting, and delegated Security Council powers including emergency action. | Uses adult-human membership rather than token holdings, separates specified human offices, and distinguishes amendment from identity-changing refounding. Digital constitutions and emergency councils already exist. |
| [Anthropic's Constitutional AI](https://www.anthropic.com/news/constitutional-ai-harmlessness-from-ai-feedback) | Model training through principles, self-critique, revision, and AI-generated preference feedback. | Addresses institutional authority and contested power across humans and systems. Model behavior and legitimate institutional authorization are distinct questions; the approaches can be complementary. |
| [LLM Constitutional Multi-Agent Governance — de Curtò and de Zarzà, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13189v1) | Hard constraint filtering and utility optimization addressing cooperation, manipulation, autonomy, and fairness; the paper reports network experiments. | Specifies membership, human offices, adjudication, emergencies, succession, amendment, and refounding. The reported experimental results do not demonstrate Alvorada's effectiveness. |
| [AgentCity — Ruan and Zhang, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07007v1) | A close architectural comparator: agent legislation of operational smart contracts, software execution, human adjudication, and human-authored foundational rules. | Alvorada derives authority from a human constitutional order and reserves final constitutional judgment to its human Court; executable rules implement authority. AgentCity v1 is a specification with a planned experimental program. |
| [Constitutional governance for societies of AI agents in the built environment — Ghoroghi, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23336v1) | A research agenda treating people and artificial agents as interacting societies, covering mechanism design, verification, and infrastructure shocks. | Provides a general institutional proposal and traceable decisions across multiple contexts. The research agenda shows that the society-of-agents framing is shared, not exclusive to Alvorada. |
| [NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/) | Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage organize AI risk management, including roles, accountability, and lifecycle oversight. | Examines constitutional sources and limits of authority. It can inform the institutional architecture within which risk management occurs, while leaving technical and sector assurance necessary. |
| [Microsoft Agent Governance Toolkit](https://github.com/microsoft/agent-governance-toolkit) and [Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs) | The toolkit documents agent policy enforcement, identity, sandboxing, and reliability; OPA evaluates policy separately from enforcement. | Provides proposed authority, review, and change rules above implementation. These tools may support downstream controls; no integration or constitutional adequacy is established here. |

**The closest comparison deserves care.** AgentCity already reserves its
foundational contracts to humans and recognizes concentrated human adjudicator
power as a limitation. Human control and separation of powers alone therefore
do not distinguish Alvorada. The difference is the constitutional allocation:
Alvorada separates foundational authority, final judgment, verification,
enforcement, and selection chains, while refusing to let adjudication amend or
refound the institution. AgentCity's executable-law model differs from
Alvorada's explicit separation of legitimacy and implementation. Neither design
has established superiority through this comparison. See AgentCity v1
§§3.2–3.3 and Appendix C.1, and Alvorada Articles II–III and VII–VIII.

The source review uses the linked papers, project documentation, and official
framework descriptions. MOISE, PolicyKit, Arbitrum, Anthropic, Metagov, policy
engines, and authorization research already appear in Alvorada's
[33-entry prior-art register](constitutional-design/sources/PRIOR_ART_REGISTER.yaml).
The three linked 2026 agent-governance papers extend this explanatory comparison;
they do not retrospectively alter that register, the accepted requirements,
source-status labels, or constitutional decisions. Paper-reported findings and
project-described capabilities are not independently reproduced here. No
chronological priority or causal influence is inferred from resemblance.

### What value does Alvorada provide?

Its present value is an **inspectable constitutional design and research
framework**. The contribution is the integration and traceability of explicit
institutional choices, not ownership of the underlying ideas. Its proposed
architecture can help evaluators ask who authorizes a consequential act, who
can challenge it, who judges a dispute, and what remains valid when ordinary
institutional operation breaks down.

| Potential value | Concrete repository artifact or mechanism | What remains to be demonstrated |
|---|---|---|
| Authority architecture | Membership-rooted human ends; bounded delegation; separated authorization, adjudication, execution, and verification. | Legitimate adoption, workable staffing, and effective independence under real incentives. |
| Lifecycle coverage | Articles V–VIII address emergency, succession, interregnum, amendment, refounding, transition, and termination. | Whether procedures remain usable under time pressure, vacancies, conflict, and institutional failure. |
| Practical human agency | IV.2 examines common dependencies, comprehensibility, viable nonmachine alternatives, and effective power. | Whether people can actually understand, reject, replace, and continue without the contested system. |
| Traceable constitutional choices | Five foundational decisions, coordinated decisions, 35 accepted requirements, provision traceability, reasons, and retained dissent. | Whether independent reviewers can reconstruct disputed authority and detect omissions or contradictory interpretations. |
| Shared evaluation vocabulary | Seventeen working principles, ten protected foundational commitments, eighteen application contexts, and proposed capture exercises. | Transferability to each domain; the ninety application exercises remain proposed and unexecuted. |
| Open critique and reuse | Public records under CC BY 4.0 allow attributed examination, adaptation, and commercial reuse of original repository material. | Adoption demand, maintenance capacity, and the cost of keeping institutional and implementation records current. |

A practical use is to review an AI-assisted customer-service denial: identify
the grant and policy source; retain uncertain evidence and the customer's
objection; provide independent review; separate the remedy from verification;
and preserve pending claims if the service tool is replaced. The same analysis
can expose a human supervisor's overreach. This illustrates a use of the
principles; it does not make the service desk a constitutional institution.

A reasonable near-term adoption path is a bounded governance-design review,
research collaboration, or principle-inspired pilot under existing lawful
authority. Full v0.1 adoption is a separate institutional undertaking. Alvorada
can guide the authority requirements for downstream engineering, but it is not
presently an enforcement product, compliance certification, or commercial
performance claim. Market value and willingness to pay remain unmeasured.

### Costs, limits, and the next evidentiary step

Full adoption requires multiple independent human bodies, qualified selectors,
records, participation, and workable review. That creates staffing and
coordination costs, potential delay, and a risk that formal separation conceals
shared funding, infrastructure, or social influence. The design can also fail
through inaccessible participation, interpretive disagreement, unavailable
reviewers, weak enforcement, or dependency on the system producing its evidence.
More offices and records do not by themselves produce legitimacy or resilience.

The repository's checks establish conformance to encoded design and integrity
invariants. They do not establish that an operating institution resists capture
or delivers better outcomes. Full constitutional adoption may be unsuitable
for a small organization or unnecessary for low-consequence tasks. Existing
corporate, public, clinical, contractual, and military duties need independent
analysis; a constitutional analogy cannot replace them.

The strongest next evaluation would compare the same bounded workflow under
an existing authority-and-review baseline and an Alvorada-inspired design.
Specify outcomes before evaluation: unauthorized effects, unresolved scope,
retention of dissent, time and accessibility of review, independent evidence
reconstruction, correlated control, replacement feasibility, service quality,
and governance cost. Include independent adversarial reviewers and outage or
vacancy cases. Report failures and tradeoffs; do not compare a paper design
with a deployed competitor using headline feature counts.

**Positioning supported by this review:** an open, traceable constitutional
research architecture for durable human/AI institutions, with explicit human
foundational sovereignty and institutional-lifecycle safeguards. The particular
combination merits further comparison and testing. Claims of being the first,
unique, complete in practice, or proven to preserve sovereignty are unsupported.


## Design lineage

Alvorada preserves a staged lineage:

> SOURCE -> ISSUE -> ALTERNATIVES -> REVIEW -> HUMAN DECISION ->
> CONSTITUTIONAL REQUIREMENT -> CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISION ->
> SUBORDINATE GOVERNANCE LAW -> IMPLEMENTATION STANDARD ->
> FORMAL REPRESENTATION -> VALIDATION / ENFORCEMENT

The stages are separated to prevent common category errors. Evidence does not
select a design. Analysis does not make a human decision. A decision does not
write its own provision. A provision does not implement itself. Executable
policy does not create the legitimacy it is intended to represent.

Not every item requires every artifact type, but skipped stages must not be
silently implied. Unresolved states, dissent, contrary evidence, correction,
supersession, and provenance remain first-class records rather than being
erased when a later decision prevails.

## Governance layers

The intended hierarchy is:

```text
Constitutional identity / sovereignty
                |
                v
Constitutional law
                |
                v
Subordinate governance law
                |
                v
Implementation standards
                |
                v
Formal machine-readable representations
                |
                v
Runtime validation / enforcement
```

Lower layers may implement and constrain conduct under higher layers. They
cannot manufacture their own legitimacy or enlarge their own jurisdiction. A
machine enforcement mechanism does not become sovereign because it is
deterministic, automated, cryptographically verified, widely relied upon, or
operationally necessary. Likewise, a human operator does not obtain
constitutional authority merely by controlling the mechanism.

## What remains

### A. Human institutional activation

- complete initial Schedule O staffing through eligible human principals and
  alternates;
- create six attributable appointment records and obtain the required
  confirmations, including acceptance, without inferred consent;
- make an eventual explicit Human Constitutional Authority ratification
  decision; and
- satisfy the Constitution's later activation and initialization conditions.

These steps are human institutional acts, not documentation or software tasks.

### B. Subordinate governance law

The current backlog includes candidate filing and service acceptance; notice,
calendars, scheduling, and reasonable filing periods; ballot, count, recount,
and publication administration; human sortition ceremony and audit procedure;
conflict and dependency disclosures; evidence intake and response procedure;
panel summons, recusals, replacements, and hearing order; term, vacancy,
alternate, successor, and transition administration; expedited case procedure
and public reporting; and bounded administration of Schedule O, Verification,
and Enforcement.

Subordinate law may make these rules administrable. It may not change the
constitutional electorate, selector, result rule, burden, threshold, trigger,
separation, or change category it implements.

The proposed [governed-agency Phase H work package](constitutional-design/organic-governance/GOVERNED_AGENCY_DESIGN_REQUIREMENTS.md)
adds delegation-expansion and lifecycle design, practical human-agency and
replacement requirements, and [capture-review cases](constitutional-design/organic-governance/CAPTURE_TAXONOMY_AND_TESTS.md).
These remain unadopted design proposals. The separate
[synthetic moral-status research issue](constitutional-design/future-research/IR-19-synthetic-moral-status.md)
does not reopen v0.1 or change its ratification prerequisites.

### C. Implementation

Implementation work remains open for identity and authentication;
eligibility proof; credentials and signatures; tamper-evident storage and
archives; voting, counting, recount, and publication systems; sortition and
randomness tooling; notifications and accessibility; evidence capture and case
management; dependency inventories and effective-power audit support;
enforcement and remedial systems; telemetry and recovery; and
transition, restoration, restitution, compensation, and claims systems.

Unresolved implementation questions authorize no runtime behavior. Software,
formal models, credentials, and tests remain subordinate to valid human
authority and constitutional law.

## How to read Alvorada

1. Read this overview, then the
   [Constitution v0.1 proposal](constitutional-design/drafts/CONSTITUTION_v0.1.md).
2. Read the [constitutional-design overview](constitutional-design/README.md)
   to understand the staged method and record classes.
3. Review [CDR-001](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-001.yaml) through
   [CDR-005](constitutional-design/decisions/CDR-005.yaml), followed by
   [CDD-001](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-001.yaml) through
   [CDD-004](constitutional-design/decisions/CDD-004.yaml).
4. Follow the [consolidated requirement map](constitutional-design/CONSOLIDATED_REQUIREMENT_MAP.yaml)
   and [traceability record](constitutional-design/drafts/CONSTITUTION_v0.1_TRACEABILITY.yaml)
   from decisions into constitutional sections.
5. Examine the [source and prior-art records](constitutional-design/sources/)
   and [history boundary](history/ORIGIN.md).
6. Read the [ratification review](constitutional-design/RATIFICATION_REVIEW_v0.1.md),
   [ratification packet](constitutional-design/RATIFICATION_PACKET.md), and
   [initial Schedule O](constitutional-design/INITIAL_SCHEDULE_O.yaml).
7. Inspect the [validator](tools/validate_bootstrap.py) and
   [mutation tests](tests/test_bootstrap.py) to see which invariants are
   machine-checked.

Run the existing checks with:

```powershell
python tools\validate_bootstrap.py
python -m unittest discover -s tests -v
python -m ruff check tools tests
```

Passing tests demonstrate conformance to encoded repository invariants. They
do not establish truth, legitimacy, human consent, ratification, security, or
production readiness.

## Contributing and reviewing

Useful contributions include:

- identifying internal or cross-article contradictions;
- adding carefully sourced historical, institutional, or technical prior art;
- performing adversarial review and capture analysis;
- documenting governance failure modes and effective-power risks;
- proposing subordinate-law procedures within settled authority boundaries;
- developing formal representations without treating them as authority;
- adding mutation tests and validation cases; and
- proposing implementation approaches with explicit assumptions and limits.

Contributions should preserve source quality, uncertainty, dissent,
supersession, and provenance. Claims should distinguish observation,
interpretation, recommendation, human decision, constitutional requirement,
constitutional text, and implementation.

A pull request does not create constitutional authority. Repository
contribution is not constitutional standing. Maintainer access is not
sovereignty. AI-generated agreement is not human ratification. Code that
successfully enforces a rule does not prove that the rule is legitimate.

## Project boundaries

Alvorada is:

- not a claim that artificial intelligence should govern humans;
- not a claim that code is law;
- not merely a model constitution, system prompt, or output policy;
- not a completed or authorized production governance runtime;
- not a claim that formal verification, cryptography, or deterministic
  execution equals constitutional legitimacy;
- not an adopted Constitution yet;
- not a populated constitutional institution; and
- not dependent on legacy named AI personas or experimental role identities.

The project is an open constitutional-engineering effort: ambitious about
institutional rigor, conservative about claims of authority, and explicit
about the distance between a validated design and a legitimate operating
institution.
