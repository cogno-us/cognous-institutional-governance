# Application catalogue: principles and constitutional coverage

**ILLUSTRATIVE APPLICATION GUIDANCE — NO CONSTITUTIONAL EFFECT**

Each context file applies all seventeen [working design principles](../docs/DESIGN_PRINCIPLES.md)
(W01–W17) and all ten refounding-level protections in Constitution VIII.1
(F01–F10). The identifiers below are documentation labels, not new constitutional
requirements. They do not alter the 35 accepted requirements, office structures,
thresholds, or decision records.

## Three forms of application

**Principle-inspired practice:** an existing organization can use the distinctions
to improve delegation, review, records, and human control under its own lawful
authority. Such practice does not mean the organization has adopted Alvorada.

**Institutional adaptation:** a separately authorized design process can evaluate
which architecture is suitable. A CEO, clinician, public official, or commander
cannot simply be renamed Human Constitutional Authority. A complaints team is
not thereby the Constitutional Court. Published statements and workflow controls
are not substitutes for legitimate constituencies and independent selection.

**Constitutional adoption:** applying v0.1 as an actual constitution requires its
human membership, seven-human College, nine-human Court, separated Verification
and Enforcement, Schedule O, selection chains, thresholds, ratification, and
activation. Changing these through a small-sector analogy would be a different
architecture requiring the appropriate constitutional change analysis. None of
the context files claims those conditions have been met.

Existing law, professional duties, contracts, patient consent, democratic
mandates, and lawful chains of command remain independently applicable. The
framework neither supplies legal powers nor replaces sector-specific assurance.

## All seventeen working principles

| ID | Working principle | Constitutional connection and interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| W01 | Govern power rather than substrate. | I.1(2,22–25), II.1, IV.2: examine both human and artificial power; human-only foundational allocations still apply. |
| W02 | Capability does not create authority. | I.1(22–25): technical access and ability cannot enlarge a grant. |
| W03 | Intelligence does not create authority. | I.1(22–25): superior performance and competence cannot originate authority. |
| W04 | Recommendation does not create authorization. | I.1(22–23), IV.1(1.a,e): recommendations and attributable grants are separate. |
| W05 | Delegation does not transfer foundational sovereignty. | I.1(22,25), V.3, VI.1: scoped autonomy never inherits institutional ends. |
| W06 | Machine validation does not itself create legitimacy. | IV.1(4), III.4(19): conformity reports cannot establish legitimate authority. |
| W07 | Human institutional authority may itself require constitutional constraint. | I.1(2), II.1, III.1: human origin does not immunize ordinary acts from review. |
| W08 | Unknown and unresolved states must remain representable. | IV.1(1.d): preserve unresolved uncertainty; this is not a prescribed private state model. |
| W09 | Material dissent should not disappear because a contrary decision prevails. | IV.1(1.d,3), VIII.3(10–11): retain material dissent through correction and change. |
| W10 | Formal authority and effective power may diverge. | I.1(24), IV.2: audit practical dependencies and control separately from titles. |
| W11 | Governance should be proportional to consequence and reliance. | III.4(2,18), V.2(1.d–e), IV.2: proportional remedies and emergency action support consequence-sensitive practice. This does not relax fixed constitutional procedures. |
| W12 | Constitutional development requires provenance. | IV.1, VII.2(10), VIII.3(10–11): retain decisions, authority sources, reasons, and supersession. |
| W13 | Artificial intelligence recommendations are not human constitutional decisions. | III.1(2), II.3(9), VII.1(3): AI assistance cannot authorize foundational change or final constitutional judgment. |
| W14 | Institutional functions should not silently collapse into one another. | II.3, III.1–III.4: separate authorization, judgment, execution, evidence, and verification. |
| W15 | Human constitutional supervision requires meaningful comprehensibility. | IV.1(2), IV.2(4–5): humans must independently understand, reconstruct, reject, choose a viable nonmachine alternative, and control timing and effect. |
| W16 | Local execution success is not durable institutional state. | Operational analogy to IV.1(2–3) and VIII.3(10–11). The original principle concerns execution and institutional persistence; the Constitution prescribes durable records, not a transaction protocol. |
| W17 | Repository state is not durable until persisted and independently observable on the authoritative remote. | Repository-specific principle, applied by analogy to IV.1(2) and IV.2(3). No constitutional clause mandates Git, remote commits, or a distributed database. |

## All ten refounding-level protections

The exact controlling text is [VIII.1](../constitutional-design/drafts/CONSTITUTION_v0.1.md).
The contextual F tables ask what the protected institutional commitment would
mean in that domain. They do not declare every ordinary operational policy
change a refounding. The Court classifies; the appropriate human process
authorizes. Proposed v0.1 remains unadopted.

| ID | Protected commitment | Controlling treatment |
|---|---|---|
| F01 | Foundational institutional purpose and ends | VIII.1(1.a); I.1(1–2): a foundational replacement cannot claim ordinary continuity. |
| F02 | Ultimate human beneficiary status | VIII.1(1.b); I.1(1): institutional convenience cannot silently replace people as ultimate beneficiaries. |
| F03 | Human sovereignty over foundational ends | VIII.1(1.c,4); VII.1(10): the human membership root and human-only foundational allocation are protected. |
| F04 | Constitutional constraint of human and artificial power | VIII.1(1.d); II.1: neither human nor machine dominance defeats binding constraint. |
| F05 | Capability, reliance, or effective control cannot create authority | VIII.1(1.e); I.1(23–24): success and dependence cannot become jurisdiction. |
| F06 | AI cannot acquire foundational sovereignty through succession, delegation, emergency, adjudication, or accretion | VIII.1(1.f,4); I.1(22–25): authority cannot migrate into a system through any of these routes. |
| F07 | Protected human agency | VIII.1(1.g); IV.2(4–5): practical choice and contestation must survive formal approvals. |
| F08 | Adjudication is distinct from amendment | VIII.1(1.h); III.1(4–5), VII.1(5): a remedy or interpretation cannot rewrite the constitutional design. |
| F09 | Amendment is distinct from refounding | VIII.1(1.i); VII.1–VII.3: identity-changing discontinuity requires its own explicit process. |
| F10 | Durable provenance and non-erasure of constitutional history | VIII.1(1.j); IV.1(3), VIII.3(10–11): change preserves rather than conceals earlier authority and dissent. |

## Coverage of every constitutional section

This index ensures that principles are considered alongside the Constitution's
actual institutions and processes. Context files apply these articles to domain
scenarios; they do not reproduce or replace the 24 sections.

| Section | What application must examine |
|---|---|
| I.1 | Human ends, the membership-rooted College, and non-accretion of authority. |
| II.1 | Binding constraint of all ordinary human and artificial institutional power. |
| II.2 | Who qualifies for membership and standing; operational stakeholder labels do not change the adult-human membership test. |
| II.3 | Separated functions, bounded initialization, and vacancy rules. |
| III.1 | Human final constitutional judgment, jurisdiction, and limits on creating authority. |
| III.2 | Independent selectors, qualifications, terms, removals, and Schedule O. |
| III.3 | Conflicts, recusal, panels, appeal, and the whole-Court conflict fallback. |
| III.4 | Bounded remedies, distinct Enforcement and Verification, and nonmanagerial reviewer powers. |
| IV.1 | Attributable authorization, reasons, alternatives, dissent, dependencies, time limits, correction, and records. |
| IV.2 | Real independence, disclosed dependencies, viable nonmachine alternatives, and effective-power audits. |
| V.1 | Explicit prior human emergency delegation as the ordinary source. |
| V.2 | Conjunctive human-only necessity fallback; no machine-created eligibility. |
| V.3 | Minimum stabilization, prohibitions, earliest expiry and the 24-hour outer fallback limit. |
| V.4 | Contemporaneous record, filing within six hours of stabilization, review within 24 hours of filing, burden and remedies. |
| VI.1 | Only existing valid grants survive where permitted; continuity creates no sovereign successor. |
| VI.2 | Predesignation, independent proof, human recognition, and separation from selection. |
| VI.3 | A predefined human fallback cannot invent a new selector or successor. |

| VIII.2 | Proper creation, composition, accountability prerequisites, and expiry of transition authority. |
| VIII.3 | Bounded custody, obligations, assets, reliance, remedies, and preserved history. |

## Cross-sector reading and evidence limits

The application controls are proposed design examples derived from Alvorada;
they are not claims of current compliance or validated deployments. The
[NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/) provides
an adjacent voluntary risk-management structure organized around Govern, Map,
Measure, and Manage. Its risk-management outcomes do not determine constitutional
legitimacy or replace Alvorada's authority questions.

[WHO's health-AI governance guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)
provides sector-specific ethical context. The
[UK Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub)
provides an example of publishing how and why public-sector algorithmic tools
are used. The [DoD Responsible AI strategy](https://media.defense.gov/2024/Oct/26/2003571790/-1/-1/0/2024-06-RAI-STRATEGY-IMPLEMENTATION-PATHWAY.PDF)
is an adjacent military-governance reference. None certifies Alvorada, and these
links are not a complete jurisdictional requirements review. Reviewed 2026-10-03.

All acceptance cases in the context files are **PROPOSED — NOT YET EVALUATED**.
No private cognitive implementation, executable policy, or sector-specific
operational command system is published here.
