# Leadership studies: institutions beyond the leader

**LATER COMPARATIVE RESEARCH — NOT ORIGINAL DESIGN PROVENANCE**

Added 2026-10-04 at the maintainer’s request. Five selective studies examine how leadership interacts with authority, constraint, dissent, inclusion and institutional continuity. The unit of comparison is a decision or mechanism, not an overall ranking of leaders. Success in one setting and harm in another can coexist.

## What the comparison adds

**Personal excellence cannot supply institutional legitimacy.** A capable or persuasive leader still needs the actual grant, required approvals and constraints. The same boundary applies to AI competence: neither successful recommendations nor technical superiority create sovereignty. See I.1 and W02–W06.

**Formal independence can conceal practical dependence.** Evidence access, suppliers, appointments, budget and agenda control determine whether review is usable. A human signature and an organizational chart do not settle effective power. See IV.2 and W10.

**Inclusion requires examining actual participation.** Mission statements and expansions of access can coexist with exclusion. Identify affected people, standing, usable challenge and the distribution of burdens; do not treat majority support as every person’s consent. See II.2 and W07.

**Emergency competence and emergency constraint must work together.** A system that blocks every protective action can fail, as can one that lets urgency erase boundaries. Evaluate lawful action, prohibited expansion, delay, expiry, review and completed remedies together. Article V supplies the proposal’s specific rules, not a generic exception derived from history.

**Reform and recovery need evidence of delivery.** An enactment, compelling explanation, rescue or pilot result is not durable institutional success. Record implementation capacity, observed effects, retained failures and outstanding obligations. See III.4, IV.1 and W16–W17.

## Method and source boundaries

1. Select episodes relevant to institutional mechanisms; pair a constructive lesson with a failure or limit. Selection is purposive, not a representative sample of leadership or world history.
2. Prefer primary records for what a leader said or an instrument provided, and institutional historical accounts for context. Speeches establish statements, not their factual accuracy, motives, public reception or causal effectiveness.
3. Link the source at the historical observation. Distinguish that observation from present interpretation and a proposed application. Primary sources are themselves situated records and require criticism.
4. Map lessons to existing provisions and working principles. No provision is adopted or amended by this comparison. The studies offer review questions, not historical causal proof that the proposal would have worked.
5. Preserve contrary evidence and attribution limits. Do not make an administration’s collective work solely a leader’s accomplishment, convert uncertainty into certainty, or compare dissimilar jurisdictions as if they had identical rules.
