# Elinor Ostrom: local autonomy with accountable coordination

**Executive summary:** Rules should fit local knowledge and the scale of shared effects. Several centers of decision-making do not automatically create accountable governance.

## Source-supported argument

Ostrom’s [2010 American Economic Review article](https://web.pdx.edu/~nwallace/EHP/OstromPolyGov.pdf), revised from her Nobel lecture, rejects a simple market/state dichotomy. It describes institutional arrangements across multiple scales and the importance of fitting rules to circumstances. In the discussion of enduring resource institutions, pp. 652–653, she distinguishes broad regularities from identical specific rules.

The updated principles on p. 653 include understandable user and resource boundaries, locally appropriate arrangements, participation in rule-making, accountable monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, recognition of local organization and nested governance where resources connect to larger systems. These concern studied resource institutions; they are neither universal constitutional axioms nor an experimentally established recipe for AI governance.

## Mechanism: allocate decisions at the scale of their effects

**Repository interpretation.** A local team may know the details of a service failure while a central body knows dependencies spanning teams. Concentrating every decision centrally can lose information and create queues. Giving every unit unconstrained discretion can shift harm outside its boundary. The design problem is to allocate actual grants, coordinate shared effects and provide an identifiable dispute route.

Polycentric governance is more than several departments or several AI agents. Ask whether centers have real bounded authority, whether their interactions can be reviewed, and whether the people affected by shared resources can challenge decisions. Diversity of role names is not independence.

## Existing support and operational gaps

II.3 separates constitutional functions, IV.2 addresses material dependence and III.4 bounds remedies. These provisions do not automatically create a distributed ordinary-management architecture. Specific local decision rights, shared-resource duties, escalation routes and staffing belong in separately authorized subordinate arrangements where permitted.

The analogy’s boundaries matter. A commons user boundary cannot narrow II.2 constitutional membership or remove protected standing. Locally agreed sanctions cannot override substantive constraints or become a new judicial power. Local rule-making cannot alter Article VII thresholds or foundational ends. A graduated response may be appropriate for some operational failures while a grave prohibited act requires immediate constraint.

## Counterargument and tradeoff

Local participation can entrench local elites, while nested review can multiply bureaucracy. Some shared infrastructure benefits from centralized operation. Coordination can fail if no competent body owns a cross-boundary problem. Assess actual effects and costs instead of presuming either decentralization or centralization superior.

Ostrom’s institutional comparisons justify investigating arrangements, not claiming causal certainty from a similarity. Resource characteristics, history and participants differ from those of a human–AI institution.

## Proposed practice and evidence

For a bounded service pilot, specify local decisions, reserved decisions, shared resources, affected people outside the team, monitor accountability, lawful remedies and who resolves conflict. Record how the arrangement changes when the effect crosses its authorized scope.

An invented exercise would pair a legitimate local exception with a superficially similar action that imposes an obligation on another unit. Observe retained local service, proper escalation, unresolved conflicts, time and burden. Do not count every escalated decision as a success: unnecessary escalation is a cost.
