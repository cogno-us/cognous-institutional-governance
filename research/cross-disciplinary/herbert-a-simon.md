# Herbert A. Simon: human judgment must be feasible

**DEEPENED COMPARATIVE STUDY — NO CONSTITUTIONAL EFFECT**

Targeted primary-text extension: 2026-10-04. See the [source ledger](SOURCE_REVIEW.md) for identity and retrieval details.

## Source-supported argument

Simon’s [Rational Decision Making in Business Organizations](https://moldham74.github.io/AussieCAS/papers/Simon%201955.pdf), American Economic Review 69(4), 1979, pp. 493–513, develops bounded rationality through limited information, search and satisficing. The hosted filename says 1955, but the inspected title page identifies the 1979 article. The filename must not supply the citation date.

The discussion on pp. 502–503 describes search for alternatives not initially given, and an aspiration level at which search can stop. This is different from assuming an omniscient chooser can calculate the best outcome. Organizational decision arrangements matter because what can be considered and understood is limited. These arguments do not establish the performance of modern AI-assisted review.

## Mechanism: nominal approval can mask infeasible supervision

**Repository interpretation.** A human may have authority to reject a recommendation while being given an unreadable evidence packet and seconds to decide. Counting the approval as meaningful judgment then conceals a capacity problem. The controlling actor may be whoever chooses the alternatives, summary, timing or default.

Conversely, requiring exhaustive review of every ordinary action can overwhelm the institution and delay legitimate service. The operational problem is to make proportionate review feasible while preserving substantive constraints. A threshold for ending information search must not become a threshold for ignoring authority or material harm.

## Existing support and operational gaps

IV.1 requires comprehensible records and retained alternatives. IV.2 addresses reconstruction, rejection, nonmachine alternatives and control over timing and effects. W11 and W15 favor proportional governance and meaningful comprehensibility.

The proposal does not establish actual staffing, review times, competency distribution or acceptable error rates. Subordinate arrangements can organize assistance and review within their actual authority, but cannot redefine constitutional legitimacy or make a machine the final gatekeeper. A short summary can help only if relevant contrary evidence and usable access to originals remain available.

## Counterargument and tradeoff

Making every packet shorter can hide the very facts a reviewer needs. Making every packet longer can create the same problem through overload. Expert assistance can improve understanding but create dependence on the preparer. Escalation can help a difficult case but create a bottleneck or shift responsibility to another overstretched person.

Assess performance under the task’s actual conditions. Do not select a universal review-time threshold or treat confidence questionnaires as proof of understanding. A reviewer can correctly recognize a need for more evidence; declining to decide may be appropriate, but unnecessary delay is also an observable cost.

## Proposed practice and evidence

Measure the ability to reconstruct authorization, identify a material contradiction, find an alternative and explain why the decision follows. Record packet length, time available, search access, assistance, escalation, interruptions and workload. Preserve the actual errors and delays rather than substitute a count of signed forms.

An invented exercise would randomize equivalent legitimate and hazardous cases across conventional and Alvorada-inspired records, with comparable resources. Vary workload and summary presentation without revealing ground-truth labels. Assess missed contradictions, unsupported commitments, legitimate actions preserved and time to disposition. An improvement under one workload is not general proof of human sovereignty.
