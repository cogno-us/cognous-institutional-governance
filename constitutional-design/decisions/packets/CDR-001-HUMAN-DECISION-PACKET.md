## Existing Architectures

### Architecture A — Unconstrained Foundational Sovereign
**Constitutional model:** A recognized human foundational sovereign controls
institutional ends and may issue, suspend, revise, or replace institutional
rules without a superior adjudicator or entrenched limit. Restraint exists
only through self-restraint, convention, transparency, resistance, or
practical dependence, all of which remain revocable or nonbinding.

**Strongest argument for:** It offers the clearest human source, the simplest
authority chain, and the fastest final action.

**Strongest argument against:** It supplies no reliable internal protection
against arbitrary action, self-serving emergency power, or unilateral waiver
of safeguards.

**Catastrophic failure mode:** Capture or abuse of the sovereign becomes
capture of the institution while formally valid commands destroy practical
means of correction.

**Principal unresolved question:** What could make any announced limit
credible against the actor entitled to rewrite or waive it?

### Architecture B — Constitution-Bound Sovereign Without External Adjudicator
**Constitutional model:** A human foundational sovereign retains control of
institutional ends while publicly binding ordinary power to constitutional
limits. The same sovereign remains the final interpreter, so text, reasons,
publication, precedent, and internal review constrain conduct without an
independent contrary judgment.

**Strongest argument for:** It combines human sovereignty with a legible
public commitment and procedural regularity without creating a separate
final reviewer.

**Strongest argument against:** The constrained actor remains final judge of
its own limits and can turn fidelity into self-serving interpretation.

**Catastrophic failure mode:** Accumulated interpretations convert binding
limits into discretionary permission while preserving the appearance of a
constitutional order.

**Principal unresolved question:** When the sovereign interprets its own
constraint, what distinguishes binding law from organized self-restraint?

### Architecture C — Constitution-Bound Sovereign With Independent Review
**Constitutional model:** A human foundational sovereign controls
institutional ends while an independent human review function judges whether
exercises of power conform to constitutional limits. Review may stay,
invalidate, or remedy ordinary action, but it does not control institutional
purpose or refounding.

**Strongest argument for:** Independent review can turn stated limits into
contestable and enforceable constraints through standing, reasons, remedies,
and separated enforcement.

**Strongest argument against:** Reviewer capture, precedent, enforcement
control, and recursive legitimacy can create a new concentration of power.

**Catastrophic failure mode:** Reviewers or their infrastructure acquire
effective control of constitutional meaning and block the human sovereign
while claiming only to interpret limits.

**Principal unresolved question:** How can review bind an ordinary sovereign
act without the reviewer becoming sovereign over institutional ends?

### Architecture D — Distributed Foundational Authority
**Constitutional model:** Foundational human authority over institutional
ends is allocated among multiple constituent bodies or communities rather
than one ordinary actor. Jurisdiction, concurrence, veto, subsidiarity,
rotation, and mutual monitoring constrain unilateral exercise while
preserving constituent autonomy.

**Strongest argument for:** Distribution reduces catastrophic single-point
capture and creates plural human counter-power.

**Strongest argument against:** Deadlock, latency, diffuse responsibility,
local capture, and informal recentralization can prevent coherent action.

**Catastrophic failure mode:** A dominant coalition or shared coordination
platform recentralizes effective power while formal fragmentation obscures
responsibility and blocks correction.

**Principal unresolved question:** Which human units possess legitimate
constituent standing, and what makes their aggregation binding?

### Architecture E — Reserved Human Sovereignty + Entrenched Constitutional Limits
**Constitutional model:** Humans collectively retain authority over
institutional purposes, existence, legitimate amendment boundaries,
refounding, and termination. Entrenched limits govern ordinary human and
machine exercises of institutional power, with adjudication and enforcement
remaining constituted rather than sovereign.

**Strongest argument for:** It states the central distinction most directly:
human sovereignty over ends can coexist with binding limits on ordinary
human and machine power.

**Strongest argument against:** Entrenchment creates rigidity, recursive
refounding questions, enforcement conflict, and risk that formal human
control becomes nominal.

**Catastrophic failure mode:** A captured review and technical apparatus
freezes a defective order, treats its own interpretation as permanent, and
makes legitimate human refounding practically impossible.

**Principal unresolved question:** Who identifies a legitimate human
refounding act when the entrenched order and its reviewers contest that act?

## Core Decision

The decision is centered on this exact distinction:

**HUMAN SOVEREIGNTY OVER ENDS** does not necessarily imply
**UNCONSTRAINED HUMAN EXERCISE OF INSTITUTIONAL POWER.**

The foundational human constituency decides the institution's purposes,
whether it exists, the original allocation of authority, and whether limits
are intended to bind ordinary exercises of power. It also decides which
changes count as legitimate amendment, refounding, or termination.

That decision does not yet settle implementation details. It does not by
itself choose reviewers, remedies, voting rules, thresholds, emergency
procedures, succession machinery, validators, offices, or technical controls.
Those mechanisms must follow, and remain subordinate to, the foundational
allocation and the chosen binding character of its limits.

## Elimination Analysis

This analysis is advisory only. No architecture is automatically eliminated,
and this packet declares none eliminated.

Architecture A appears constitutionally weakest and dominated for the stated
objective because it provides no reliable internal constraint. It risks
becoming structured sovereign discretion rather than constitutional
governance: every safeguard remains waivable by the actor it is meant to
constrain.

Architecture B remains coherent, especially where publicity, reasons,
regularity, and political accountability are expected to discipline the
sovereign. It remains weak against self-dealing and self-interpretation
because the party whose power is challenged gives the final answer.

Architectures C, D, and E remain materially distinct and are not dominated by
current repository evidence. C emphasizes independent review, D distributes
foundational authority, and E reserves human sovereignty while entrenching
limits on constituted power. Their failure modes and legitimacy burdens
differ too substantially for automatic elimination.

## Recommended Architecture

**Recommendation:** Architecture E

**Boundary:** RECOMMENDATION_ONLY — NO CONSTITUTIONAL EFFECT

Architecture E is the clearest fit for the stated objective because it
preserves explicit human sovereignty over purpose, original authorization,
legitimate amendment, refounding, and termination while refusing to equate
that sovereignty with every ordinary human command. It permits humans to
create a binding order in which human officeholders, reviewers, operators,
and artificial intelligence systems exercise governed authority rather than
inherent authority.

The recommendation is substantive, not neutral. Architecture A cannot
reliably deliver the requested internal constraint. Architecture B makes
constraint depend too heavily on the constrained actor's final
interpretation. Architecture C strengthens review but leaves the foundational
status of entrenchment less explicit. Architecture D reduces single-point
capture but changes the foundational allocation by distributing sovereignty,
which is a separate constituency choice rather than merely a constraint
mechanism. Architecture E most directly preserves the requested distinction
while allowing useful mechanisms from B, C, and D to be considered later.

Its advantage is not that formal entrenchment guarantees legitimate outcomes.
Its advantage is that it exposes the necessary layers: foundational human
choice, ordinary constituted authority, amendment, refounding, adjudication,
enforcement, and technical implementation. That structure makes bypass,
capture, and category mistakes more contestable.

**Strongest objection:** Entrenchment may become intolerably rigid; the
legitimacy of amendment and refounding becomes recursive when the existing
order disputes the authority or constituency attempting change; and effective
power may transfer to reviewers, operators, or technical infrastructure even
while formal sovereignty remains human. If those risks cannot be bounded,
Architecture E could preserve a captured order more effectively than it
preserves meaningful human sovereignty.

## Hybrid Question

### FOUNDATIONAL ARCHITECTURE

The recommended foundational architecture is Architecture E. Any hybrid
should retain its allocation: humans hold sovereignty over institutional
ends, legitimate amendment, refounding, and termination, while ordinary
institutional power remains governed by binding limits.

### IMPLEMENTATION / INSTITUTIONAL MECHANISMS

Candidates for later incorporation, without changing that foundational
sovereignty allocation, are:

- From Architecture C: independent human review, with review intensity,
  standing, enforcement, and remedy still unresolved.
- From Architecture D: plural or distributed counter-power, independent
  verification, local refusal within granted scope, and resistance to a
  single coordination monopoly.
- From Architecture B: publication, reason-giving, explicit precedent and
  supersession, durable dissent, and procedural regularity.

These are implementation or institutional mechanisms, not alternative
holders of foundational sovereignty. Their competence, appointment,
independence, scope, correction, and failure handling remain later questions.

### Rejected Mechanism Classes

- Machine sovereignty or final machine adjudication.
- Reviewer control over institutional ends or refounding.
- Ordinary-command bypass of entrenchment.
- Performance or popularity as a source of authority.

## Human Decision Options

These labels prepare a later explicit human action. Packet selection, markup,
or repository presence has no effect by itself.

1. **ADOPT_A** — Choose Architecture A as the future foundational model,
   accepting ultimate human discretion without a reliable superior internal
   constraint.
2. **ADOPT_B** — Choose Architecture B as the future foundational model,
   relying on public limits, reasons, regularity, and final human
   self-interpretation.
3. **ADOPT_C** — Choose Architecture C as the future foundational model,
   constituting independent human review that can bind ordinary exercises of
   power.
4. **ADOPT_D** — Choose Architecture D as the future foundational model,
   distributing foundational human authority among legitimate constituent
   units.
5. **ADOPT_E** — Choose Architecture E as the future foundational model,
   reserving human sovereignty over ends while entrenching limits on
   ordinary governed power.
6. **ADOPT_HYBRID** — Specify the foundational architecture, every
   incorporated mechanism, and every rejected mechanism; a list of
   mechanisms without a sovereignty allocation is insufficient.
7. **REVISE_AND_REVIEW** — Direct further analysis or packet revision before
   any foundational architecture is chosen, preserving all options pending
   another explicit review.
8. **DEFER** — Take no architecture position now and keep FQ-01 unresolved
   until a later explicit human process.

## Decision Questions

1. Which human constituency ultimately holds sovereignty over institutional
   purpose, original authorization, refounding, and termination?
2. May an ordinary foundational human act be invalid because it violates an
   entrenched constraint, even when its human authors sincerely claim
   sovereign authority?
3. What review and remedy may bind a sovereign's ordinary violation without
   allowing the reviewer, enforcer, or validator to become sovereign?
4. Which changes remain amendment, which require refounding, and how much
   entrenchment may legitimately bind future humans?
5. During emergency, succession failure, or interregnum, who holds residual
   power, and may any artificial intelligence system ever hold a
   foundational office?

## Consequences for Later Design

### Consequence of ADOPT_A

- IR-06 would center final adjudication in the sovereign; IR-03 would permit broad
  residual emergency discretion; IR-04 would depend on designation, recognition,
  or effective control. IR-15 would collapse amendment and refounding into
  sovereign choice, IR-17 would retain a mutable hierarchy, and IR-10 separation
  would constrain subordinates but not the sovereign. Later work would still
  have to expose capture and practical irreversibility.

### Consequence of ADOPT_B

- IR-06 would use internal review and final sovereign interpretation; IR-03 could
  specify triggers, expiry, reasons, and restoration without an independent final
  check. IR-04 would gain written rules but retain recursive interpretation;
  IR-15 and IR-17 would require legible change and source procedures; IR-10
  separation would remain advisory or subordinate at the foundational level.

### Consequence of ADOPT_C

- IR-06 would require an independent human forum, standing, review force, and
  remedy; IR-03 would require rapid or retrospective review. IR-04 would need
  continuity for both sovereign and reviewer; IR-15 would distinguish adjudication
  from amendment and refounding; IR-17 would locate review in the hierarchy;
  IR-10 would require meaningful separation of review and enforcement from the
  reviewed actor.

### Consequence of ADOPT_D

- IR-06 would require a joint, cross-level, or independently constituted dispute
  forum; IR-03 would allocate provisional local and common emergency powers.
  IR-04 would preserve redundancy while defining withdrawal and local succession;
  IR-15 would require constituent participation; IR-17 would express jurisdictions
  and concurrence; IR-10 would separate functions across units without hiding
  shared infrastructure.

### Consequence of ADOPT_E

- IR-06 would need human adjudication capable of binding ordinary acts without
  controlling ends; IR-03 would remain narrow, temporary, reviewable, and unable
  to bypass entrenchment. IR-04 would separate office continuity from constituent
  loss; IR-15 would define amendment and refounding; IR-17 would distinguish
  foundational, entrenched, ordinary, and technical layers; IR-10 would require
  separated functions where constraint depends on them.

### Consequence of ADOPT_HYBRID

- Later design would be constrained first by the expressly named foundational
  architecture, then by the incorporated and rejected mechanisms. IR-06, IR-03,
  IR-04, IR-15, IR-17, and IR-10 would each need a traceable allocation showing
  which architecture supplies sovereignty and which mechanisms supply review,
  emergency action, continuity, change, hierarchy, and separation. Incompatible
  combinations would remain unresolved until explicitly revised.

### Consequence of REVISE_AND_REVIEW

- No architecture would be constrained yet. IR-06, IR-03, IR-04, IR-15, IR-17,
  and IR-10 must all remain open, as must constituency, review force, emergency
  scope, succession, entrenchment, hierarchy, and separation. Revision should
  identify the requested new evidence or normative comparison without implying a
  default architecture.

### Consequence of DEFER

- No architecture would be constrained yet, and no option would become a default
  through delay. IR-06, IR-03, IR-04, IR-15, IR-17, and IR-10 must remain open;
  operational work must not silently settle adjudication, emergency power,
  succession, refounding, source hierarchy, or separation; those questions
  remain outside both this packet and the later FQ-01 decision.

## Material Ambiguities

- **Constituency:** The repository does not identify the legitimate ultimate human
  constituency, its membership, standing, aggregation rule, or continuity.
- **Review force:** It does not settle whether review advises, stays, invalidates,
  remedies, or triggers amendment or refounding, nor how the reviewer is constrained.
- **Emergency and succession:** It does not allocate residual power during imminent
  harm, incapacity, interregnum, contested succession, or loss of constituent humans.
- **Amendment-refounding boundary:** It does not identify entrenched values, change
  thresholds, unamendable commitments, or evidence of a legitimate refounding.
- **Effective power:** It does not ensure that formal human control will survive
  dependency on reviewers, agenda setters, operators, identity systems, validators,
  or shared infrastructure.
- **Machine formalization and legitimacy:** It does not settle which rules can be
  formalized, how indeterminacy is represented, or how technical acceptance remains
  subordinate to human constitutional legitimacy.
