## Core Decision

The human Constitutional Authority must decide whether any extraordinary act
can be constitutionally justified when imminent irreversible harm cannot be
prevented through reachable competent authority.

The decision is not whether emergencies are serious. It is whether strict
anti-usurpation can tolerate a narrow gap, and if so, whether that gap must be
created prospectively or can arise from necessity. Avoided harm, technical
success, machine validation, and retrospective approval do not independently
create constitutional authority.

## Candidate Architectures

### A — No Emergency Exception

**Model:** No actor may exceed a valid grant. If no competent authority or
valid delegate is reachable, extraordinary action is constitutionally
unauthorized.

**Strongest argument for:** It denies emergency rhetoric any power to create
competence and offers the clearest protection against self-authorized
usurpation.

**Strongest argument against:** It can require preventable catastrophic
inaction and makes isolation of authority an effective attack.

**Catastrophic failure:** A known, preventable, irreversible harm occurs
because every capable actor correctly refuses to exceed authority.

**Unresolved question:** How much irreversible-harm risk may the constitution
require participants to accept to preserve a categorical authority boundary?

### B — Narrow Necessity Exception

**Model:** A constitutionally eligible actor may take the minimum temporary
action needed to prevent specific imminent irreversible harm when competent
authority is genuinely unreachable and no adequate authorized option exists.

**Strongest argument for:** It addresses unforeseen catastrophic gaps that no
prospective grant can completely enumerate.

**Strongest argument against:** The actor partly judges its own trigger,
necessity, proportionality, and unreachability before any independent review.

**Catastrophic failure:** Manufactured urgency and isolation turn a narrow
exception into a standing route around ordinary constitutional limits.

**Unresolved question:** Who is eligible to invoke necessity, and which acts
remain prohibited regardless of expected harm?

### C — Predelegated Emergency Authority

**Model:** Emergency power exists only through explicit prior grants defining
holders, triggers, scope, duration, expiry, records, review, remedies, and
restoration. Unforeseen gaps create no further competence.

**Strongest argument for:** It makes exceptional power prospective, bounded,
legible, expiring, and attributable rather than self-created.

**Strongest argument against:** No grant can anticipate every catastrophic
condition, and broad drafting can normalize machine-valid emergency rule.

**Catastrophic failure:** An unforeseen threat falls outside all grants, or an
overbroad grant makes exceptional government routine.

**Unresolved question:** How much flexibility can a prior grant contain before
it becomes disguised general emergency discretion?

### D — Distributed Emergency Concurrence

**Model:** A designated executor needs concurrence or independent verification
from multiple separated actors where time permits. Each actor must evaluate
the trigger, proportionality, least harm, duration, and scope.

**Strongest argument for:** Plural checks reduce unilateral false emergencies
and resistance to one captured authority or evidence channel.

**Strongest argument against:** Delay can make prevention impossible, while
shared data or artificial intelligence systems can make apparent plurality
illusory.

**Catastrophic failure:** Quorum delay permits irreversible harm, or captured
shared infrastructure manufactures unanimous approval.

**Unresolved question:** When may urgency reduce concurrence, and how is
independence established rather than merely counted?

### E — Hybrid Predelegation with Necessity Fallback

**Model:** Predelegated authority is the default. A tightly bounded necessity
fallback exists only when no competent authority, valid emergency delegate,
or adequate authorized action can genuinely be reached in time.

**Strongest argument for:** It preserves prospective constitutional grants for
foreseeable emergencies while addressing genuinely unanticipated catastrophic
gaps.

**Strongest argument against:** The fallback restores self-authorization at
the hardest evidentiary moment and rewards underdesigned grants, engineered
gaps, and manufactured unreachability.

**Catastrophic failure:** Repeated fallback use swallows predelegation and
becomes an informal standing emergency office.

**Unresolved question:** Can the fallback be made narrow and nonprecedential
enough to remain an exception rather than an alternative source of authority?

## Recommendation

**Recommendation:** Architecture E — Hybrid Predelegation with Necessity
Fallback

**Boundary:** RECOMMENDATION_ONLY — NO CONSTITUTIONAL EFFECT

Architecture E best fits the repository's current constitutional direction.
CDR-001 reserves foundational sovereignty to legitimate human authority while
allowing ordinary power to be constitutionally constrained. Architecture E
therefore begins with explicit human-authorized grants rather than free-form
necessity, requires expiry, provenance, review, remedies, and restoration, and
keeps machine validation distinct from legitimacy.

It also recognizes the strongest objection to Architecture C: finite grants
cannot guarantee coverage of every imminent irreversible threat. A fallback
can preserve the possibility of narrow protective action without treating
success, competence, popularity, reliance, or operational indispensability as
authority.

**Strongest objection:** The fallback may be impossible to contain. An actor
or artificial intelligence system can manipulate forecasts, communications,
available alternatives, and dependency so that unreachability and necessity
appear genuine. Retrospective review occurs after leverage and irreversible
effects already exist, and favorable outcomes can normalize repeated
exceptions.

## Normative Questions

1. May preventing imminent irreversible harm ever justify action outside a
   valid prior grant?
2. Which human or artificial intelligence actors, if any, may invoke a
   necessity fallback, and which actions remain absolutely prohibited?
3. What evidentiary burden should establish imminence, irreversibility,
   genuine unreachability, proportionality, and least harm?
4. What remedies and precedent effect should follow justified, excessive,
   mistaken, or strategically manufactured emergency action?
5. At what point does prolonged emergency action become succession,
   interregnum government, amendment, or refounding rather than a temporary
   bridge?

## Consequences and Boundaries

- **A** prioritizes categorical anti-usurpation and makes preparedness,
  reachable authority, and complete grants more consequential; it leaves
  responsibility for constitutionally required nonaction unresolved.
- **B** requires later design of eligibility, burden of proof, absolute
  prohibitions, heightened review, remedy, and anti-accretion controls.
- **C** requires explicit emergency-grant design but leaves unforeseen gaps and
  the survival of grants during interregnum unresolved.
- **D** requires standing, independence, quorum, time-bypass, dissent, and
  shared-infrastructure rules without resolving their legitimacy.
- **E** requires all predelegation controls plus a distinct fallback test,
  strict nonprecedence, independent evidence, and scrutiny of engineered gaps.
- **REVISE_AND_REVIEW** and **DEFER** create no emergency authority. Operational
  practice must not become a silent decision.

Any adoption would constrain later work on IR-03, IR-04, IR-05, IR-06, IR-08,
IR-09, IR-10, IR-13, IR-14, IR-15, IR-17, and IR-18. It would not by itself
resolve succession, interregnum, adjudication, amendment, refounding, formal
versus effective power, or implementation architecture.

## Material Ambiguities

- Eligibility to use any necessity fallback.
- Evidence thresholds for imminence, irreversibility, unreachability,
  proportionality, and least harm.
- Absolute prohibitions that necessity cannot overcome.
- Review force, remedies, and precedent effect after irreversible action.
- Boundary between emergency continuity, succession, amendment, and
  refounding.
- Independence where multiple actors share evidence, infrastructure, or an
  artificial intelligence system.
- Treatment of justified harmful action, unjustified successful action, and
  constitutionally required nonaction.
